Stock finance for trading businesses

For wholesalers, distributors, importers, manufacturers and retailers who have to buy stock before they can sell it. Here’s how it works, what lenders look for, and what it costs.

What stock finance is

Stock finance — also called inventory finance or a stock facility — is a credit line secured against the goods your business holds. The lender values your eligible stock and lets you borrow a percentage of that value. As you sell stock and buy more, the amount available moves with it.

That makes it different from a business loan. A loan gives you a lump sum that you pay back over a fixed term. A stock facility is revolving: you draw it when you need to buy, repay it as you sell, and draw it again for the next order.

How a facility works, step by step

  1. The lender assesses your stock. They look at what you hold, how fast it sells, and what it would fetch if they had to sell it themselves.
  2. A limit is agreed. You’re offered a facility based on a percentage of eligible stock value — often around 50–70% for finished goods.
  3. You draw funds to buy. Typically the lender pays your supplier directly, or advances against stock you already hold.
  4. You repay as you sell. When goods are sold, the funding against them is repaid and that headroom becomes available again.
  5. You report regularly. Most lenders want a monthly stock report, and will carry out audits or valuations from time to time.

What lenders look for

Not all stock is equal in a lender’s eyes. The questions they ask are simple: could they sell it, how quickly, and for how much?

  • Finished, saleable goods are the easiest to fund. Raw materials and part-finished work are funded at lower rates, if at all.
  • Turnover of stock matters. Goods that sell in weeks are more attractive than slow lines that sit for a year.
  • Perishable, bespoke or fashion stock is harder, because its value can fall quickly.
  • Good stock records make a real difference. A lender needs to trust your figures.
  • Your trading history and accounts still count, as do the directors’ track record and personal guarantees.

This is where a broker earns their keep. Each lender has different appetites for stock types and sectors. A full review of your figures up front means your case goes to the lenders most likely to fund it, presented in the way they want to see it.

What it costs

Pricing varies a lot between lenders and depends on the risk. Before you sign, you should understand each of these:

ChargeWhat it is
Interest or discount chargeCharged on the amount you’ve drawn, usually expressed as a margin over Bank of England base rate or as a monthly rate.
Arrangement feeA one-off fee to set up the facility, often a percentage of the limit.
Service or facility feeAn ongoing fee for running the facility, sometimes charged whether you use it or not.
Audit and valuation feesThe cost of the lender inspecting and valuing your stock, at the start and periodically.
Exit or early termination feesCharges for ending the facility before the minimum term.

Stock finance and invoice finance together

Stock finance covers you up to the point of sale. If you sell on credit terms, invoice finance can then release cash against the invoice instead of waiting 30, 60 or 90 days to be paid. Used together, they fund the whole trading cycle — and some lenders offer both in one facility. Invoice finance is AIM’s lead product, so we can look at the two side by side. Related ways to fund stock

  • Trade and import finance pays overseas suppliers before goods arrive, often by letter of credit or direct supplier payment.
  • Purchase order finance funds the supplier costs of a specific large order you’ve won.
  • Commercial buy now, pay later extends your payment terms with suppliers.
  • Revolving credit facilities and business loans can suit if your stock isn’t a good fit for asset-based lending.
