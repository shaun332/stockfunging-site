Stock finance vs invoice finance: which does your business need?

By Shaun Elliott, Director of AIM Financial Solutions. November 2026.

Both release cash that’s tied up in your trading cycle. The difference is where in the cycle the cash is stuck: stock finance helps before you sell, invoice finance helps after.

The short answer

  • Stock finance lends against goods you hold or are about to buy. It suits businesses that have to pay suppliers before their goods are sold.
  • Invoice finance lends against invoices you’ve raised to business customers. It suits businesses that wait 30, 60 or 90 days to be paid.
  • If you have both problems — paying for stock up front and waiting to be paid — you may need both, and some lenders combine them in one facility.

Where the cash gets stuck

Think about a wholesaler that buys £100,000 of stock from a supplier who wants paying on order. The goods take six weeks to arrive and another six weeks to sell. The wholesaler then invoices its trade customers on 60-day terms.

From paying the supplier to being paid by customers is around five months. For all that time, £100,000 of the business’s cash is out of reach. Stock finance funds the first part of that gap. Invoice finance funds the second.

How stock finance works

A lender values your eligible stock and lets you borrow a percentage of it — often around 50–70% for finished goods. As you sell stock and buy more, the facility revolves. Lenders care most about how saleable your stock is: fast-moving, finished goods are easiest to fund, while raw materials, perishables or bespoke items are harder. How invoice finance works

Once you’ve delivered goods and raised an invoice, a lender advances most of its value straight away — commonly up to 80–90%. You receive the balance, minus the lender’s charges, when your customer pays. There are two main forms:

  • Invoice factoring: the lender runs your sales ledger and collects payment from your customers.
  • Invoice discounting: you keep control of collections, and your customers don’t need to know a lender is involved.

Invoice finance is AIM’s lead product. You can read more about factoring and discounting on our main site.

When to use both

Using the two together makes most sense when your business both holds significant stock and sells on credit — distributors, wholesalers and manufacturers supplying trade customers are the classic examples. Funding the whole cycle means you can accept larger orders without running out of cash halfway through.

Some lenders offer both as one asset-based lending facility, which can be simpler to run and may give you a larger total facility than two separate products. Others are stronger in one than the other. Which is better depends on your mix of stock and debtors, and that’s worth looking at with your actual figures.

Questions to ask before you choose

  1. Where is my cash tied up for longest — in stock, or in unpaid invoices?
  2. How quickly does my stock sell, and could a lender easily resell it?
  3. Do I sell to businesses on credit terms, or mostly to consumers?
  4. Will my funding need grow with stock levels, with sales, or both?
  5. Would one combined facility be simpler than two separate ones?
